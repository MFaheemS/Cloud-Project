\begin{abstract}
Edge--cloud placement systems decide where large language model (LLM) inference
runs using a throughput figure measured on a cool, idle device. On consumer GPUs
that figure degrades under sustained load as the device throttles itself, and
temperature, the signal such systems usually monitor, settles long before the
degradation ends. In preliminary single-run measurements on an RTX~3050 laptop
GPU, sustained throughput fell by up to 43\%, and the 21\% cold-start speed
advantage of a 4-bit over an 8-bit quantized model disappeared at thermal
equilibrium. This study characterises sustained performance across model sizes,
quantization levels and two GPUs, tests the mechanism behind the loss of
quantization advantage, builds a predictor of sustained throughput from a short
measurement, and evaluates a placement policy driven by it.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Running LLMs on local and edge devices is increasingly attractive for cost and
privacy. Deciding \emph{where} a request should run---on the device or in the
cloud---depends on an estimate of how fast the device is. That estimate is
normally taken once, from a short benchmark on cool hardware.

\section{Problem and Motivation}
A consumer GPU running continuously heats up and reduces its own clock speed to
protect itself. It keeps reporting a normal temperature and keeps working, but it
does substantially less work than the benchmark suggested. A placement decision
based on the cold figure can therefore become wrong within minutes, and nothing
in the system detects it.

Quantization, which shrinks a model so it needs less memory and arithmetic, is
one of the main techniques that make edge inference practical. It is almost
always evaluated in short runs, so any interaction between quantization and
sustained thermal behaviour is invisible to current evaluations.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}
\paragraph{Primary research question.} How does quantization level affect the
throughput a consumer GPU can sustain under continuous LLM inference, and can
steady-state performance be predicted from a short measurement well enough to
make correct edge--cloud placement decisions?

\paragraph{Objectives.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Characterise peak and sustained throughput, energy per token, and heating
        and cooling behaviour across model sizes, quantization levels and two GPUs.
  \item Explain the mechanism behind the loss of quantization advantage by
        separating the effect of reduced clock speed from heat itself.
  \item Build and validate a predictor of sustained throughput from a short
        cold-start measurement.
  \item Evaluate a placement policy driven by that predictor against policies
        that assume constant performance or react to temperature.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Edge measurement studies.} Sustained-load benchmarks on mobile, NPU and
laptop GPU platforms confirm heavy throttling under continuous inference
\cite{tummalapalli2026edge}, but test a single model at one quantization level,
are measurement-only, and do not consider placement. Evaluations of quantized
models on edge hardware report energy, accuracy and latency
\cite{husom2025sustainable}, but not how those trade-offs change over a long
session.

\paragraph{Energy benchmarks.} Large cross-GPU energy studies
\cite{fadelargerich2026wattcounts} characterise many models and architectures but
use only 16-bit weights, list quantized models as future work, and measure
steady state without a time axis.

\paragraph{Thermal- and energy-aware management.} Thermal- and power-aware
schedulers exist for datacentres \cite{stojkovic2025tapas}, relying on facility
cooling and power-capping controls that consumer hardware does not expose.
On-device energy managers \cite{zou2026enerinfer} tune a single device rather than
deciding where work should run.

\paragraph{Placement and precision.} Edge--cloud schedulers
\cite{yang2024perllm} assign requests using performance estimates measured in
advance, and dynamic-precision methods \cite{liu2025flexquant} switch quantization
level for accuracy or resource targets without considering heat.

\section{Research Gap}
No existing work measures how quantization level changes a device's ability to
sustain performance under continuous load, or connects that behaviour to the
decision of where inference should run.

\section{Methodology}

The study proceeds in four stages: we reproduce the baseline to establish that
our harness is faithful, characterise sustained-load behaviour that the baseline
does not measure, formulate an estimator for the quantity the baseline reports
incorrectly, and separate the contribution of its components by ablation.

\subsection{Baseline reproduction}
We reproduce the accuracy results of Husom et al.~\cite{husom2025sustainable}
using the authors' released inference and evaluation scripts without
modification to prompts, scoring or protocol. Each configuration is evaluated on
the authors' pre-sampled 200-prompt files with five repetitions
(\mbox{1\,000} generations per cell) at temperature~0, matching their
\texttt{exp.sh}. Accuracy is a property of the model, its quantization and the
prompt, and is independent of the host device; it is therefore the appropriate
axis on which to verify fidelity across differing hardware. Throughput, power
and latency are \emph{not} compared to the published values, because the
original study instruments a Raspberry~Pi~4 with an external hardware power
meter whereas this work measures a discrete GPU through NVML.

\subsection{Sustained-load measurement}
Short benchmarks observe a device only in its cold, unthrottled state. To
characterise behaviour under continuous service we define a three-phase
protocol. Each run first idles until the GPU falls below a fixed cold-start
threshold of $60\,^\circ$C, then applies 15~minutes of uninterrupted inference,
then records 15~minutes of idle recovery. Device temperature, power draw, SM
clock frequency and the NVML throttle-reason bitmask are sampled at 20~Hz, and
decode throughput is recorded per request.

Controlling the cold start is essential rather than cosmetic: an uncontrolled
pilot that began at $88\,^\circ$C, already throttling, measured only half the
degradation of an identical run started cold, because it never observed peak
throughput. We also record the engine's CPU/GPU offload split, since a model
that does not fit in VRAM is partially CPU-resident and occupies a different
operating regime that must not be pooled with GPU-resident configurations.

\subsection{Proposed estimator}
Let $y(t)$ denote decode throughput at elapsed time $t$ under continuous load,
and let $y_\infty$ denote its steady-state value. A short-window benchmark
reports $\bar{y}_W$, the mean over an initial window of length $W$, as though it
estimated $y_\infty$. On a thermally constrained device the two differ
systematically.

We instead model the observed decay as first-order,
\begin{equation}
y(t) \;=\; a + b\,e^{-t/\tau},
\label{eq:decay}
\end{equation}
and report $\hat{y}_\infty = a$. For a fixed $\tau$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:decay} is
linear in $(a,b)$, so the estimator grid-searches $\tau$ over $20$--$600$\,s and
solves the two-parameter least-squares problem in closed form at each step,
retaining the fit with the lowest residual sum of squares. The procedure is
deterministic, adds no dependencies beyond the standard library, and completes
in milliseconds, so it can run inside an existing benchmark harness rather than
as a separate offline analysis.

The estimator is deliberately decomposed into two independent components, so
that their contributions can be attributed separately:
\begin{description}
  \item[A --- sustained measurement.] Extend the measurement window from $W$ to
    a longer horizon $L$ and report the mean of its final 60\,s. This buys
    accuracy with wall-clock time and requires no model.
  \item[B --- decay extrapolation.] Fit Eq.~\eqref{eq:decay} and report its
    asymptote instead of a window mean. This requires no additional measurement
    time.
\end{description}

\subsection{Ablation design}
Because the proposed method introduces two components, an improvement over the
baseline does not by itself show which component is responsible. We therefore
evaluate the four arms of Table~\ref{tab:arms}, all computed from the same raw
telemetry so that the comparison is like-for-like:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Ablation arms. $W=60$\,s, $L=300$\,s.}
\label{tab:arms}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Arm & Estimate of $y_\infty$ & Measurement cost \\
\midrule
Baseline & $\bar{y}_W$, mean of first $W$ & $W$ \\
$+$A     & mean of final 60\,s of $L$ & $L$ \\
$+$B     & $a$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:decay} fitted on $[0,W]$ & $W$ \\
$+$A$+$B & $a$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:decay} fitted on $[0,L]$ & $L$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The baseline arm reproduces exactly what the original short-run protocol
reports, so the original system remains available for comparison throughout.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
Ground truth $y_\infty$ is taken as the mean throughput after 700\,s of
continuous load, by which point the device has reached thermal equilibrium.
Estimator quality is reported as mean absolute percentage error
\[
\mathrm{MAPE} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\frac{\lvert \hat{y}_{\infty,i} - y_{\infty,i}\rvert}{y_{\infty,i}}\times 100,
\]
together with the worst-case error across configurations and the measurement
cost in seconds, since an estimator that is accurate only after a long
measurement is of limited practical value. Reproduction fidelity is assessed
with a two-proportion $z$-test between our accuracy and the published value.

The original study reports no Q8\_0 or FP16 results for the 1.5B model, as
neither fits on its 4\,GB Raspberry~Pi. Measuring that region gives
CommonsenseQA accuracies of 0.698 (Q8\_0) and 0.705 (FP16) against 0.593 for
Q4\_K\_M: a 10.5-point gain from Q4 to Q8 that then plateaus.

\subsection{Sustained-load behaviour}
Under continuous load every GPU-resident configuration degrades substantially
(Table~\ref{tab:sustained}). Device temperature is a poor indicator of this:
it reaches equilibrium roughly nine times faster than throughput does, so the
device appears stable throughout the period in which most performance is lost.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Peak and sustained throughput (RTX 3050 Laptop, 15\,min load).}
\label{tab:sustained}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Configuration & Peak (tok/s) & Sustained (tok/s) & Loss & Steady clock \\
\midrule
0.5B Q4 & 243.6 & 179.9 & 26.2\,\% & 1181\,MHz \\
1.5B Q4 & 115.8 &  66.2 & 42.9\,\% &  839\,MHz \\
1.5B Q8 &  95.4 &  66.8 & 30.0\,\% &  832\,MHz \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Notably, Q4 and Q8 converge to a common floor: the 21\,\% cold-start throughput
advantage of Q4 over Q8 is absent at thermal equilibrium, so the speed benefit
of aggressive quantization does not survive sustained operation even though its
accuracy cost does.

\subsection{Estimator evaluation and ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports estimator error over the three GPU-resident
configurations. The short-window baseline overstates sustained throughput by
51\,\% on average and by 75\,\% in the worst case. Both proposed components
reduce this, and they are not redundant: component~B alone matches the accuracy
of component~A at one-fifth of the measurement cost, and combining them halves
the error again.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Ablation. Error is against measured steady state; FP16 excluded as
partially CPU-bound.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Arm & MAPE & Worst case & Cost \\
\midrule
Baseline & 51.1\,\% & 75.0\,\% &  60\,s \\
$+$A     & 18.1\,\% & 27.7\,\% & 300\,s \\
$+$B     & 17.9\,\% & 39.2\,\% &  60\,s \\
$+$A$+$B & \textbf{10.5\,\%} & 23.3\,\% & 300\,s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The improvement is confined to performance characterisation: the method does
not alter model outputs, so accuracy is unchanged from the reproduced baseline.
